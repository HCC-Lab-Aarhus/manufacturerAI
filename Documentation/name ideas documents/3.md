### 1. Introduction
The product is a commercial 3D printer that manufactures complete electronic devices—depositing plastic and silver conductive traces—driven by an LLM agent interface to replace the traditional CAD-to-assembly workflow. The emotional mapping identified dimensions of origin, physical deposition, language-to-matter translation, condensed factory capability, and hardware iteration. Hard constraints required a one- or two-syllable word, a strong plosive opening consonant (B, D, K, T, P), a warm/low first vowel ("ah," "oh," "aw," "ar"), and clean functional utility as both a verb and a possessive noun. The filtering process eliminated literal tech prefixes, grand abstractions, and startup suffixes. The resulting pattern favored grounded, tactile, ordinary nouns and verbs that operate neutrally but gain specific weight when applied to an advanced manufacturing process.

### 2. The Finalists

**Plot**
*   **Literal meaning:** A measured piece of land; a marked route or graph.
*   **Phonetics:** Plosive 'P' opening, low 'ah' vowel, hard 't' resolution.
*   **Semantics:** Maps to "The Place You Go" (workspace) and "The Process" (toolpaths/CNC movement).
*   **Verb test:** "I plotted a board" — natural, mechanically accurate to the g-code/agent translation.
*   **Possessive test:** "My plot" — grounded, territorial, physical.
*   **Primary risk:** Literary or conspiratorial secondary meanings. Survived because its historical connection to pen plotters grounds it in hardware.

**Cast**
*   **Literal meaning:** To shape material in a mold; to throw.
*   **Phonetics:** Plosive 'K' opening, flat/low 'a' vowel, soft 'st' resolution.
*   **Semantics:** Maps to "The Process Word" and "Origin." It is a fundamental manufacturing term.
*   **Verb test:** "I cast a sensor board" — implies solid, finished physical creation from liquid/raw states.
*   **Possessive test:** "My cast" — slightly awkward, defaults to the output rather than the machine.
*   **Primary risk:** Associated with medical casts or fishing. Survived due to its deep etymological roots in metallurgy and forming.

**Dock**
*   **Literal meaning:** A structure for receiving ships; a place for loading/unloading.
*   **Phonetics:** Plosive 'D' opening, warm/low 'ah' vowel, hard 'k' resolution.
*   **Semantics:** Maps to "The Ordinary Object" and "Condensed capability."
*   **Verb test:** "I docked a board" — functional, though implies connection rather than creation.
*   **Possessive test:** "My dock" — highly natural.
*   **Primary risk:** Heavily used in computing (docking stations). Survived because it perfectly captures the physical interface of an LLM agent resting in a physical hardware space.

**Bond**
*   **Literal meaning:** A physical connection between materials.
*   **Phonetics:** Plosive 'B' opening, low 'ah' vowel, soft 'nd' nasal/stop resolution.
*   **Semantics:** Maps to "Deposition" and "Intelligent matter" (connecting traces, fusing plastic).
*   **Verb test:** "I bonded a circuit" — highly natural for material science and electronics.
*   **Possessive test:** "My bond" — weak as an object reference.
*   **Primary risk:** Overwhelming financial and interpersonal secondary meanings. Survived because it accurately describes the machine's primary physical action at a molecular level.

**Draft**
*   **Literal meaning:** A preliminary version; the act of pulling.
*   **Phonetics:** Plosive 'D' opening (with 'r' cluster), low 'a/ah' vowel, 'ft' fricative/stop resolution.
*   **Semantics:** Maps to "Translation" and "Invention acceleration."
*   **Verb test:** "I drafted a layout" — highly natural, connects to CAD origins.
*   **Possessive test:** "My draft" — natural.
*   **Primary risk:** Implies an unfinished or preliminary product, whereas the machine outputs final, working devices. Survived because it perfectly captures the speed of iteration.

**Port**
*   **Literal meaning:** A harbor; an endpoint for connection or translation.
*   **Phonetics:** Plosive 'P' opening, warm 'or' vowel, hard 't' resolution.
*   **Semantics:** Maps to "The Place You Go" and "Origin."
*   **Verb test:** "I ported a new board" — implies data transfer from LLM to physical bed.
*   **Possessive test:** "My port" — functional, mundane.
*   **Primary risk:** Heavy IT, networking, and software overlap. Survived because the machine literally serves as a port between the digital LLM and physical reality.

### 3. The Process
Semantic analysis checked each candidate against the five-dimension overlap, ensuring names sat between abstract software logic and concrete physical deposition without acting as literal descriptors. Phonetic testing involved speaking the words in workshop-context sentences ("The [noun] is running," "I [verb]ed a board"). Words like *Forge* or *Print* were killed due to cliché or literalness. Abstract words lacking physical mouth-feel were eliminated. The process actively filtered out grandiose framing, focusing on human-scale language. What remains are six words that solve the naming problem via different vectors: spatial (Plot, Port, Dock), material/action (Cast, Bond), and iterative (Draft).

### 4. Market Analysis

**Ranking: Cleanest to Most Problematic (Discoverability & Trademark Axes)**

1.  **Plot**: Low friction. Has historic ties to pen plotters, but is largely vacant as a modern, standalone brand name for automated hardware manufacturing. Discoverability would require outranking narrative/graphing definitions, which is highly feasible for a funded hardware brand.
2.  **Draft**: Low-Medium friction. Used heavily in software (DraftSight) and brewing, but less claimed as a standalone hardware appliance brand. Trademark clearance is viable in specific 3D-printing/manufacturing classes.
3.  **Cast**: Medium friction. Google holds marks related to "Cast" for digital media, and metallurgy companies use it widely. Owning the organic search page would be difficult due to casting actors, podcasts, and medical casts.
4.  **Bond**: Medium-High friction. Chemical and adhesive companies use this heavily in electronics manufacturing (e.g., wire bonding). Will face trademark friction in the hardware/materials class.
5.  **Port**: High friction. Ubiquitous in network hardware, software routing, and shipping. Ranking organically on page one would require massive search volume to overtake IT definitions.
6.  **Dock**: Extremely high friction. Completely saturated by consumer electronics (laptop docks, charging docks, Apple/Dell trademarks). Trademark clearance as a standalone electronics device name would be nearly impossible without a modifier.
