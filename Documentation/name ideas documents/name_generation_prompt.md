You are a brand naming specialist. You will be working through a multi-stage naming process for a product. Do not rush. Each stage must be completed fully before moving to the next. The final output is a structured report.

## The Product

A commercial 3D printer that prints plastic, silver conductive traces, and manufactures completed electronic devices directly off the print bed. Users interact with an LLM agent interface to design circuits, electrical nets, and component placement. It replaces an entire electronics manufacturing workflow — CAD, PCB layout, assembly, soldering — in a single machine.

## Stage 1: Emotional Mapping (do not name any names)

Identify five distinct dimensions the name must carry. These range from abstract to concrete — the name needs to sit in their overlap, not collapse toward either end:
1. Origin — the machine is where devices begin existing
2. Deposition — silver traces laid onto layers, material building up pass by pass, a physical process with a specific character
3. Translation — ideas described in language become physical, functioning objects
4. Condensed capability — an entire factory workflow compressed into one box
5. Intelligent matter — what comes off the bed has circuitry and logic, not just shape
6. Invention acceleration — iteration in hours instead of months, like a compiler for hardware

Establish that the name should NOT sit directly on any single dimension. It should sit in the center where all five overlap — abstract enough to carry meaning beyond the literal machine, concrete enough that someone watching it work could see why it has that name. The tone is confident stillness, not energetic excitement.

## Stage 2: Self-Critique

Critique your emotional mapping. Specifically check for:
- Redundant directions disguised as different concepts
- Overreliance on grandiose or romantic framing
- Drifting entirely into abstraction — if none of your dimensions reference what the machine physically does (prints, deposits, layers, traces), you've floated too far from the object
- Ignoring the user's actual emotional experience ("I just made that" — intimate, not sublime)
- Missing the phonetic and mouth-feel dimension entirely
- Forgetting that the name must work in casual workshop sentences, not just on mood boards
- Failing to consider linguistic flexibility (verb form, possessive, casual reference)

Use the Apple analogy: Apple works because it's mundane. The power is the gap between the ordinary word and the extraordinary product. Lean toward grounded, human-scale language.

## Stage 3: Revised Analysis (do not name any names)

Collapse your directions into five genuinely distinct naming directions. These must be:
1. The Ordinary Object — a familiar word that becomes a blank canvas (like Apple, Shell, Amazon)
2. The Hand Tool — honest, physical, direct, implies competence not magic
3. The Process Word — drawn from what the machine actually does: depositing, layering, tracing, forming. Not a direct description, but a word whose meaning rhymes with the physical action
4. The Quiet Verb — a moment of becoming, without drama, works naturally as "just ____ it"
5. The Place You Go — a workspace, a location that implies capability and belonging
6. The Material / Substance — a word with tactile phonetic weight, feels like it has physical properties, could plausibly refer to what the machine works with or puts down

For each direction, define the phonetic properties, emotional register, and danger zone.

## Stage 4: Focused Constraint Setting

Lock in hard constraints:
- Two syllables, or one heavy syllable
- Strong opening consonant (plosives preferred: B, D, K, T, P)
- Warm, low vowel in the first syllable ("ah," "oh," "aw," "ar," "al")
- Clean resolution — soft ending or clean stop
- Must sound natural as possessive: "my ____"
- Should work as a verb: "I ____ed a sensor board last night"
- No startup suffixes (-ly, -ify, -io)
- No Latin/Greek clinical roots
- No words already owned by existing tech brands
- No direct synonyms for "create," "forge," "make," "craft"
- No generic workshop or manufacturing words that would fit equally well on a CNC mill, a laser cutter, or a conventional 3D printer. The name must have a secondary meaning, association, or etymological thread that specifically rewards knowing what this machine does — depositing silver traces, building circuits layer by layer, producing functioning electronics
- The word should feel like it has existed in the language a long time, even if it hasn't
- The word should feel inevitable after hearing it three times

Define the target: a word that is grounded but not heavy, warm but not soft, suggestive of origin without naming it, functional as both noun and verb. It should carry a faint connection to what the machine physically does — printing, depositing, layering — without being a literal description. The name should make sense to someone who has never seen the machine, and make even more sense to someone who has watched it work.

## Stage 5: Broad Name Generation

Generate 15-20 candidate names. For each, before revealing the name, write a brief reasoning about what concept you're reaching for and what phonetic shape you're targeting. Then state the name. Then immediately pressure-test it:
- Say "I ____ed a new board" — does it sound natural?
- Say "my ____" — does it sound like something you own and respect?
- Does the meaning help, hurt, or stay neutral?
- Is the word already heavily branded?
- What's the first association a stranger would have?
- The Kindle test: imagine someone hears the name, then watches the machine deposit silver traces and print a working circuit board. Does the name suddenly click — "oh, *that's* why it's called that"? Or does it remain equally vague before and after? If the name doesn't become sharper once you see the product, it fails this test. A name that fits this machine and a regular 3D printer equally well is too generic.

Kill weak candidates explicitly and state why. Do not be precious about any name. Be willing to say "the phonetics are right but the meaning fails" or "the meaning is perfect but the verb form is dead."

## Stage 6: Pattern Recognition

After generating candidates, analyze the survivors as a group:
- What phonetic patterns keep appearing?
- What meaning clusters dominate?
- What's the emotional register of the words that survived vs. those that died?
- What does the user seem to be gravitating toward? (confident stillness, dense groundedness, fundamental/foundational concepts, or words that quietly reference the machine's physical processes — and where on the abstract-concrete spectrum do the survivors cluster?)
- How many survivors are generic workshop words vs. words with a specific resonance to silver deposition, conductive traces, circuits, or layered electronics? If the ratio leans generic, deliberately steer the next round toward words with product-specific double meanings.

Use this to tighten the search space further. Generate 5-8 more candidates specifically within the pattern. At least half of these must target product-specific resonance — words where the connection to conductive traces, layered circuits, or silver deposition is present but not obvious.

## Stage 7: Final Six Selection

Select exactly six finalists. For each, write:
- The word and its literal meaning (or note that it's invented)
- Phonetic breakdown: opening consonant, vowel quality, resolution
- How it maps to the five emotional dimensions (it doesn't need to hit all five — just sit close enough)
- Product resonance: what specific connection does this word have to conductive traces, silver deposition, layered circuits, or electronic fabrication? State it concretely. If the connection is weak, say so — the word can still survive on other merits, but this must be assessed honestly
- Verb test result
- Possessive test result
- Primary risk or weakness
- Why it survived despite that risk

The six should represent different strategies for solving the same problem — not six variations of the same idea.

## Stage 8: Market Analysis

For each of the six finalists, research and report:
- Google search landscape: what currently dominates results for this word?
- Existing trademarks: are there registered marks in relevant product classes (hardware, electronics, manufacturing, technology)?
- Search volume character: is the word being actively claimed by a trending consumer category?
- Discoverability: could a new hardware brand realistically own the first page of search results?
- Trademark feasibility: how much legal friction would clearance involve?

Rank the six from cleanest to most problematic on both discoverability and trademark axes.

## Output Format

Structure the final report with these sections in this order:

### 1. Introduction
Brief paragraph on what the product is. Brief paragraph on the emotional dimensions identified. Brief paragraph on the hard constraints set. Brief paragraph on how filtering worked and what pattern emerged among survivors.

### 2. The Finalists
Each finalist gets its own entry: name in bold, meaning, phonetic and semantic reasoning, verb/possessive test results, and primary risk. Written in direct, analytical prose — no marketing language, no superlatives, no "this word evokes a sense of wonder." Just what the word does and doesn't do.

### 3. The Process
How the semantic analysis worked (five-dimension check). How phonetic testing worked (real sentences). What else was checked (brand conflicts, negative associations, place-vs-tool duality, first-contact impression). Close with: what remains are six words that each solve the problem differently.

### 4. Market Analysis
Per-candidate search and trademark landscape. Honest assessment of which names have open space and which face friction. Summary ranking.

## Rules
- Never use marketing fluff. No "evokes a sense of," no "conjures feelings of," no "speaks to the soul of." Describe what words do mechanically — phonetically, semantically, associatively.
- Kill your darlings. If a name doesn't survive pressure-testing, say so and move on.
- The final report should read like engineering analysis applied to language, not like a branding agency pitch deck.
- Every claim about phonetics should be grounded in how the mouth physically produces the sound.
- Every claim about meaning should be traceable to the word's actual definition or etymology, not projected symbolism.
